No PRC tax on converting CNY to USD was found. The real costs are the bank spread, the U.S. §988 currency gain, and a reporting penalty you can avoid.

A U.S. citizen with a mainland bank balance is told the money is “stuck” behind a tax. The documents do not support that: the charge that looks like a conversion tax is either the bank's spread, the PRC tax on the income that created the money, or the U.S. tax on the currency gain — and only the last one is a genuine conversion tax. This page prices all three, in the order they hit you.

The short answer

  1. Move it through a bank with documents. The published China-side cost is $53.38 on $10,000 (0.5338%) and $899.01 on $400,000 (0.2248%) — the 1‰ wire tariff capped at CNY 260 plus CNY 150 cable, and a 0.2094% half-spread on the board. That is the whole bill from the Chinese side.
  2. The tax you were warned about is most likely the U.S. one. Disposing of a CNY deposit is a §988 transaction and the gain is ordinary income. At the 2024 IRS average of 7.189 CNY/USD, converting at today's ask produces a 7.0093% gain on the dollars received — $10,373.79 of federal tax at a 37% marginal rate on $400,000. It is the same for every lawful channel, so changing channel cannot avoid it.
  3. Only the first ~$2,853 escapes it. §988(e)(2) excludes a personal transaction only if the gain is $200 or less. At a 7.01% gain that ceiling is about $2,853 converted, so the exclusion is irrelevant at these amounts. No tax-treaty relief was analysed in this pass, so none is claimed (F14).
  4. The genuinely large numbers are penalties for not reporting. A non-willful FBAR violation is capped at $16,536 and a willful one at $165,353 per violation. Splitting the amount across relatives' quotas, by contrast, is a PRC offence carrying up to 30% of the amount — $120,000 on $400,000. Both dwarf the $410 bank fee people try to avoid.

The rule that decides your route. If you hold a 外国人永久居留身份证 you get the annual convenience amount for buying FX. If you do not, a foreign individual buys FX from lawful current-account RMB income with ID and source-of-funds materials including tax documents — the convenience amount is expressly limited to PR-ID holders. That is the single most consequential sentence in this research (S04). SAFE's Tianjin branch restated exactly that rule on 13 July 2026 for a foreign individual remitting labour remuneration earned in China, which is the closest official statement to your situation found in this pass (S16). It states no cap and no convenience amount for a non-PR-ID holder.

Which route applies to you — a four-question test

Question 1
Do you hold a 外国人永久居留身份证?
Yes → the annual convenience amount is open to you (M05). No → go to question 2.
Question 2
Are you emigrating and cancelling mainland household registration?
Yes → emigrant property transfer (M03), one application, tax certificate first.
Question 3
Is the balance inherited mainland property?
Yes → inheritance transfer (M04) at the deceased's registered locality.
Question 4
Is it documented current-account income?
Yes → M06/M01, the cheapest priced route. No → the balance needs a different legal characterisation before any bank will move it; that is a written branch answer, not a fee decision.

Do these five things before moving anything

  1. Get the branch's answer in writing on which rule it applies to a foreign passport holder, and on whether your documents establish lawful current-account income. S04 / S03
  2. Reconstruct your CNY cost basis by acquisition date, because that is what decides whether there is a §988 gain at all. A 2021 acquisition produces a loss at today's rate; a 2024 acquisition produces a 7% gain. Tax page
  3. Fix U.S. reporting first. FBAR if the aggregate exceeded $10,000 at any point in the year; Form 8938 if the threshold applies. The penalty caps are larger than the bank bill by two orders of magnitude.
  4. Do not split the amount across relatives' quotas. It is the exact pattern arts. 39 and 45 sanction, and the fine is a percentage of the amount.
  5. Ask for the quote, not the tariff. The tariff is published; your branch's actual rate, any local charge, and the U.S. receiving fee are not.
