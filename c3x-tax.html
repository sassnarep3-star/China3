Tax & penalty decomposition

Seven different charges get called “the tax on converting”

Only one of them is a tax on the conversion itself: the U.S. §988 currency gain. The rest are the price of the trade, a PRC tax on the income behind the money, or penalties for not reporting. They behave completely differently, so they are separated here.

1 · The U.S. §988 currency gain is the real conversion tax

26 U.S.C. §988(c)(1)(C)(i) treats the disposition of nonfunctional currency as a §988 transaction, and §988(a)(1)(A) makes the gain ordinary income. An individual's personal transaction is excluded by §988(e)(1) only under §988(e)(2) — the preceding sentence shall not apply if the gain which would otherwise be recognized on the transaction exceeds $200 (S09). The capital-gain election in §988(a)(1)(B) covers forwards, futures and options, not a bank deposit.

This is why the answer to “how do I avoid the tax” is “find your basis”, not “find a cleverer channel”. The gain is measured from acquisition to disposition. If your CNY came in when the yuan was weaker than today you have a loss, and on a personal transaction that loss is not recognised either. No route changes this arithmetic — the tax is identical whether you wire once, wire eight times, or use the emigrant route.

3 · PRC tax attaches to the income, not the conversion

The Individual Income Tax Law's nine taxable categories do not include currency conversion. It does tax property transfer income at 20%, with the base being proceeds less original value and reasonable expenses (art. 6(5)), and it requires a taxpayer cancelling Chinese household registration to emigrate to settle tax before cancellation (art. 13). S08

The practical consequence for the “considerable taxes” story. If a PRC tax appears when you try to move the money, look at what it is attached to. A 20% charge on a property sale or on interest is the income tax on the gain, and it is due whether or not you move the money offshore. A “conversion tax” with no income behind it was not found in any instrument reviewed (F09).

5 · The reporting you must not skip

  • FBAR. A U.S. person with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, foreign accounts whose aggregate value exceeds $10,000 at any time in the calendar year must file FinCEN Form 114 (S13). Caps: $16,536 non-willful, $165,353 willful, per violation, for penalties assessed on or after 2025-01-17 (S10).
  • Form 8938. A U.S.-resident unmarried specified individual crosses the threshold above $50,000 at year-end or $75,000 at any time; living outside the U.S., above $200,000 / $300,000. Penalty up to $10,000 plus $10,000 per 30 days after notice, capped at $60,000 (S12).
  • FinCEN Form 105. Carrying more than $10,000 in or out of the United States; the threshold is the family or group total, not per person; failure risks seizure and forfeiture (S14). It is a report, not a tax.
