Where the cost of converting actually comes from

Three different things are routinely conflated: the bank's conversion cost, a Chinese tax, and a U.S. tax or penalty. Only two of the three are real on the lawful route, and they are in different jurisdictions.

1 · Published bank cost
0.21% + CNY 410
Spread plus tariff. Small and published. boc.cn S21 · boc.cn S20
2 · Chinese tax on conversion
None identified
Not among the nine IIT categories. Left blank rather than guessed. shanghai.chinatax.gov.cn S11
3 · U.S. §988 + reporting
≈6.94% gain
Ordinary income, plus information-return penalties if a report is missed. govinfo.gov S14 · ecfr.gov S15

China side: what is actually penalised

The Chinese penalties attach to behaviour, never to the conversion itself: evading FX control, trading FX illegally, buying FX with false documents, and changing a declared use. Each is a percentage of the whole amount, which is why they dominate every other cost. safe.gov.cn S08

U.S. side: three separate systems

Income tax on the currency gain (§988)

Disposing of nonfunctional currency — expressly including a foreign-currency bank deposit — is a §988 transaction, and the gain is computed separately and treated as ordinary income. The capital election in §988(a)(1)(B) covers forward, futures and option contracts, not the disposition of a deposit, so the preferential long-term capital rates are not the right comparison here. govinfo.gov S14 · irs.gov S18

The personal-transaction rule of §988(e)(2) would exclude the gain, but only while the gain is at most USD 200. At today's rate that is about USD 2,883 of converted currency. On a USD 10,000 conversion the gain is $693.70, more than 3× the ceiling.

Information returns (no tax, real penalties)

The FBAR and Form 8938 do not tax anything. Missing them is where the money is lost.
