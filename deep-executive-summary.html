Executive summary

The short answer, then the evidence.

Bottom line

No official source reviewed establishes a Chinese "conversion tax" on moving your own already-owned CNY principal into USD. The costs that actually bite are (1) Chinese source-of-funds income tax and the tax-clearance documents a bank requires before it will sell you FX, (2) bank spread, wire and correspondent fees, and (3) U.S. information-reporting penalties — the largest of which is a willful FBAR penalty of up to 50% of the account balance. The cheapest-penalty strategy is therefore not a clever conversion trick: it is picking the lawful documentary channel that matches how the money was earned, obtaining a written all-in bank quote, and filing FBAR / Form 8938 / Form 3520 correctly. Informal brokers, quota splitting and crypto/USDT convert a paperwork problem into 30%–100% administrative fines plus criminal exposure.

Under the PBOC Individual Foreign Exchange Measures, Art. 37 (S05), a U.S. citizen holding a passport is an 境外个人 (foreign individual). Under SAFE Current Account Guidelines Art. 59 (S03), a foreign individual purchases FX using an ID plus evidence of the source of the RMB (including a tax certificate) — and SAFE's own Q&A states that genuine, lawful current-account FX purchase has no amount limit (S01), while the annual convenience amount is available only to holders of a foreign permanent-residence ID (S03). If the CNY is legitimately earned, tax-paid income, the USD 50,000 "annual quota" that dominates online advice may simply not be the binding constraint. This must be confirmed with the account-opening bank because the 2008 State Council regulation defines 境内个人 differently (see IR-007).

Recommended sequence (least penalty first)

1

Classify the account holder and the money. Which ID opened the account? Is the CNY salary, business profit, sale of property, inheritance, gift, or previously-converted FX? Collect bank statements, contracts, and Chinese tax receipts. Without this, no route can be selected (IR-014).

2

Ask the bank, in writing, which rule applies. Ask it to state whether it will process the request under Art. 59 (foreign individual, source-of-funds evidence, no annual quota), Art. 57 (current-account purchase above the convenience amount), or a capital-account approval route — and list the exact documents.

3

Clear the tax-document precondition. For single outbound payments above USD 50,000, a tax-filing form (税务备案表) may be required; for foreign individuals, a tax certificate / payment or exemption certificate is part of the source-of-funds evidence. This is paperwork, not a conversion tax (C16, C17).

4

Get an all-in written quote. Customer CNY/USD rate, reference mid-rate and timestamp, spread, conversion charge, wire fee, cable fee, correspondent/intermediary charges, receiving-bank fee and expected net USD. Verified published components are on the Penalty breakdown page; the account-specific total is deliberately blank (IR-012).

5

Use one lawful channel; never split. Do not use relatives' quotas, underground banks, false invoices or crypto. Compare the onshore bank route with a lawful cross-border RMB / offshore CNH route only if the bank confirms eligibility.

6

Fix U.S. reporting before and after the transfer. FBAR ($10,000 aggregate trigger), Form 8938, worldwide income (including CNY account interest), the $200 personal FX-gain rule, and Form 3520 if the funds are a foreign gift. If prior years were missed, use the IRS Streamlined or delinquent FBAR procedures (U22–U24).
