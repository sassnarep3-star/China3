1. The conversion itself is not what gets taxed

No official source read here taxes converting RMB or wiring it out. What the bank asks for is proof that Chinese tax on the money was already dealt with. If it was not, that income tax is the large number: 3% to 45% on wages, 20% on dividends, rent and property gains. Bank-deposit interest is exempt.

2. The bank's own cost is about half a percent or less

$53.42 on $10,000 (0.53%) and $901.20 on $400,000 (0.23%) at Bank of China's published rate and fee. Fees taken by other banks along the way are not published and are left blank.

S01 BOC S02 BOC · Cost tables

3. The U.S. side can cost more than the bank

Selling RMB is a currency sale under U.S. tax law. RMB acquired at the 2024 average rate would show a gain of about 6.9% today, or $27,703 on $400,000, taxed at your ordinary rate. The personal-transaction relief in §988(e) only covers gain of $200 or less, so at these amounts it does not apply. Each missed report has its own penalty: up to $16,536 for an FBAR, $10,000 for Form 8938, and 5% of a gift per month for Form 3520.

4. The route depends on where the money came from

A U.S. passport holder does not get the $50,000 a year that Chinese citizens convert on ID alone. Each lawful route is tied to a kind of money: income earned in China, a home sale, an inheritance, or money you brought in yourself. Borrowing relatives' quotas, private swaps and crypto are prohibited, with fines of up to 30% of the amount and more in serious cases.
