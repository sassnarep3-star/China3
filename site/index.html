There is no large tax on converting CNY to USD.
The large cost is a missed form.

Money in a Chinese bank account, owned by a US citizen. We traced every lawful and every prohibited route, read the statutes on both sides, and priced the whole problem. The conclusion is uncomfortable but it is well evidenced: the PRC imposes no tax on the act of converting or transferring your own funds. The penalties people experience are for not filing US information returns, or for using a route China prohibits.

The short answer

  1. Use the ordinary bank route first. USD 50,000 per person per year on ID alone, at about 0.42%. Each person may use their own quota for their own money; moving your money through their quota is structuring and is expressly penalised.
  2. Above the quota, find the real underlying event. The quota is a documentary convenience, not a legal ceiling — SAFE's own Q&A says genuine current-account conversion has no amount limit once evidenced.
  3. For an inheritance or after emigration, use the dedicated capital-account route. It is the only mechanism built to move a whole balance, and it costs paperwork rather than tax.
  4. File every US form on time. FBAR above USD 10,000 aggregate; Form 8938 above the threshold; Form 3520 Part IV above USD 100,000 from a foreign individual. These attach to the account you already hold.
  5. If filings were missed, correct them deliberately. The Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures wipe out the FBAR and information-return penalties entirely for a citizen who genuinely lived abroad; the domestic track caps the penalty at 5% of the highest aggregate balance. Against 50% for a willful violation.
  6. Do not park the money in a Chinese fund. A Chinese mutual fund is very likely a PFIC, and the default §1291 regime taxes the gain at the top rate for every year held, plus interest.
