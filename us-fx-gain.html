The short version — read this first

The "considerable amount of taxes" the user reports is most likely a U.S. tax, not a Chinese fee. Under 26 U.S.C. §988, disposing of a nonfunctional-currency bank deposit (including a CNY deposit) is a §988 transaction. If the CNY appreciated against the USD while you held it, converting it recognizes a foreign-currency gain. Every CNY you received when USD/CNY was higher is worth more USD today, and the difference is taxable.

Quantified two ways below. CNY acquired at the 2024 IRS yearly average (7.189) and converted at the recorded 2026-11-09 BOC 现汇卖出价 (6.6919) carries a built-in currency gain of ≈6.91% of the USD received. At the 2025 IRS yearly average (7.129) it is ≈6.13%. Compare: the bank spread and wire fees on the same conversion are ≈0.2–0.6% (see cost tables) — the §988 gain is roughly 10–30× the entire bank-side cost.

What this page is / is not. It is a derived, arithmetic breakdown from recorded official values (IRS yearly averages, one dated BOC board snapshot, and the U.S. Code), built to close cross-session finding X-11. It is not a tax calculation for your account: your acquisition-date basis is unknown (flagged IR-014), the tax character and even the existence of a recognized gain depend on facts only you and your accountant have. Not legal, tax, or banking advice — confirm with a qualified U.S. tax professional before converting.

2. The arithmetic, shown longhand (so you can audit it)

You hold N CNY in a Chinese bank deposit. You convert to USD at the customer rate S = 6.6919 CNY per USD.

USD received = N ÷ 6.6919 USD basis = N ÷ A (A = IRS yearly average for the year you acquired the CNY) §988 gain = N ÷ 6.6919 − N ÷ A

Dividing both sides by the USD received (N ÷ 6.6919) gives the gain as a share of the USD you walk away with:

Gain ÷ USD received = 1 − S ÷ A = 1 − 6.6919 ÷ A

If your CNY came in over several years (e.g., salary), each year's CNY has its own basis — a blended gain, usually closer to the 2024/2025 columns for recent earnings. Unrealized gain at any earlier date is not taxed; §988 applies on disposition (the conversion).

4. What actually decides whether you owe — the statutory fine print

  • §988(c)(1)(C) — bank deposits are §988 transactions. "Nonfunctional currency" includes bank demand or time deposits. So converting the CNY deposit itself is the triggering disposal, independent of any China-side approval.
  • §988(e)(2) — personal-transaction de minimis. No gain is recognized on a personal transaction if the gain does not exceed $200. If the gain exceeds $200, the exclusion does not apply at all — the full gain is recognized, not merely the excess over $200.
  • §988(e)(3) — what is "personal". A transaction is not personal to the extent its allocable expenses meet §162 (trade or business) or §212 (production of income, i.e., investment). An interest-bearing CNY deposit held for investment would generally fail the personal-transaction test — the taxpayer cannot use the $200 exclusion and, per §988(a)(1)(A), the gain is ordinary income, not capital gain. IRS Pub. 525's "report it as a capital gain" language is written for personal foreign-currency transactions; which side of the line your deposit falls on is a fact question for your accountant.
  • §988(a)(1)(A) — character. Any §988 gain/loss attributable to a §988 transaction "shall be computed separately and treated as ordinary income or loss". The Pub. 525 capital-gain statement applies only to personal transactions. Do not assume preferential capital-gain rates.
  • Losses. Personal foreign-currency losses are generally not deductible. Investment-character losses are ordinary under §988(a)(1)(A), subject to the usual ordering and limits.
  • Basis. Basis = amount of CNY paid (in USD) at acquisition, translated at the rate prevailing when the funds were acquired/received (IRS yearly average is commonly used for salary; spot-at-receipt also accepted if used consistently, per the IRS page in §1). Inherited CNY takes a fair-market-value basis; gifts take carryover basis. Wages received in CNY and already taxed in the U.S. still carry a CNY-denominated basis — the §988 gain/loss arises because the USD changed while you held the CNY.
  • Interaction with China-side routes. Whichever lawful route you use (current-account evidence, salary repatriation, emigrant-property transfer), the U.S. §988 math is identical — it is triggered by the conversion to USD, not by the China approval. Reducing the China-side fee to zero does nothing to the §988 line. What does change the §988 line: (a) converting sooner vs later (depends on future rate, unknowable now); (b) loss harvesting within the same tax year (Investment-character gains can offset ordinary losses; personal transactions cannot create deductible losses); (c) tax character, which only facts can fix.
  • Reporting. §988 gain on a personal transaction above $200 is reported on your Form 1040 (Pub. 525 directs capital-gain reporting for personal transactions). Investment-character §988 gain is ordinary income reported like other ordinary income. FBAR (FinCEN 114) and Form 8938 thresholds are independent of the §988 computation — see U.S. tax & reporting.

6. Practical takeaways (least-tax lawful handling)

  1. China side: continue using the narrowest lawful documentary route (current-account evidence, salary repatriation, or the staged emigrant-property transfer) — see methods and routes. Do not split transactions or use nominee quotas; that creates SAFE exposure that dwarfs any FX consideration.
  2. U.S. side: before converting, compute your per-year CNY basis (each year's wages, interest, inheritance) and ask a CPA to quantify and characterize the §988 gain. If the deposit is investment-character, expect ordinary income treatment and no $200 exclusion.
  3. Sequencing: converting while CNY is strong vs weak changes the gain mechanically (a weaker CNY at conversion = smaller/negative §988 gain). Timing decisions have tax consequences; they are not a reason to evade China's controls, only a reason to plan the conversion with your advisor.
  4. Reporting hygiene: FBAR at aggregate >$10,000; Form 8938 thresholds; Form 3520 for large foreign gifts — see U.S. tax & reporting. Penalties for non-filing (up to the greater of $165,353 or 50% for willful FBAR) dwarf conversion costs.
