U.S. tax and reporting obligations for a U.S. citizen with a Chinese bank account

All figures tied to official U.S. sources (IRS, FinCEN, eCFR, U.S. Code). Blank values = not confirmed by an official source.

1. Moving principal is not income — but the conversion can trigger §988

Transferring funds between your own accounts — even internationally — is not income by itself. U.S. tax liability arises on (a) the income that produced the money if it was never taxed (U.S. citizens are taxed on worldwide income, wherever they live), and (b) any foreign-currency gain under IRC §988 when the CNY is converted to USD. A CNY deposit appreciated since acquisition can carry a gain of several percent of the USD received — see the §988 exposure page for the $10,000–$400,000 arithmetic.

Sources: IRS Publication 54, Tax Guide for U.S. Citizens and Resident Aliens Abroad — irs.gov verified; 26 U.S.C. §988 — govinfo.gov verified

2. FBAR — FinCEN Form 114 (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts)

  • Who files: U.S. persons (including U.S. citizens) with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, foreign financial accounts.
  • Threshold: aggregate value of all foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any time during the calendar year (tested at the year's highest point, not year-end).
  • Where/how: filed electronically with FinCEN via BSA E-Filing (bsaefiling.fincen.gov) — not with your tax return and not to the IRS.
  • Deadline: April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15 (no form needed).
  • It is a disclosure, not a tax — filing creates no additional tax liability by itself.

Sources: IRS FBAR practice unit — irs.gov verified; IRS/FinCEN comparison — irs.gov verified; FinCEN BSA E-Filing — bsaefiling.fincen.gov verified

3. Form 8938 — FATCA (IRC §6038D)

  • Filed with Form 1040 if specified foreign financial assets exceed thresholds (broader than FBAR; includes non-account assets such as foreign stock held directly).
  • Example thresholds: single filer living abroad — >$200,000 at year-end or >$300,000 at any time; higher for married filing jointly.
  • FBAR and Form 8938 are independent — filing one does not satisfy the other.

Source: IRS, Comparison of Form 8938 and FBAR requirements — irs.gov verified

4. Foreign Earned Income Exclusion — Form 2555 (IRC §911)

  • 2025 maximum exclusion: $130,000 (inflation-adjusted annually; IRS Rev. Proc. 2024-40).
  • Requires a tax home in a foreign country plus either the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test (330 full days in a 12-month period).
  • Applies to earned income only (wages, salary, self-employment) — not investment/passive income.

Sources: IRS Rev. Proc. 2024-40, item .39 (§911) — irs.gov verified; IRS Publication 54 — irs.gov verified

5. Foreign Tax Credit — Form 1116 (IRC §901)

  • Credits Chinese income tax paid against U.S. tax on the same income, relieving double taxation.
  • Cannot double-dip: you cannot take the credit on income excluded under FEIE.
  • No U.S.–China totalization agreement exists (U.S. Social Security and Chinese social-insurance contributions can overlap).

Source: IRS Publication 54 — irs.gov verified

6. U.S.–China income tax treaty

  • Signed April 30, 1984; in force January 1, 1987.
  • Contains a savings clause (Protocol 1, ¶2): the U.S. may tax its citizens as if the treaty did not exist — most treaty benefits are unavailable to U.S. citizens.
  • Practical relief for citizens comes from FEIE and the Foreign Tax Credit, not the treaty.

Source: IRS, China tax treaty documents — irs.gov verified

7. IRC §988 — foreign currency transactions

  • §988(c)(1)(C): a nonfunctional-currency bank deposit (including a CNY deposit) is a §988 transaction — the conversion itself is the taxable disposal.
  • Personal transactions (§988(e)): no gain recognized if the gain does not exceed $200 per transaction. If the gain exceeds $200, the exclusion does not apply at all — the whole gain is recognized, not merely the excess. Personal currency losses are generally not deductible.
  • “Personal” is narrow (§988(e)(3)): a transaction is not personal to the extent allocable expenses qualify under §162 or §212. An interest-bearing CNY deposit held for investment can fail the personal test.
  • Character (§988(a)(1)(A)): §988 gain/loss is generally ordinary income or loss. IRS Pub. 525’s “report it as a capital gain” guidance is written for personal foreign-currency transactions; investment-character gains are ordinary. Which side your deposit falls on is fact-dependent — ask your accountant.

Sources: 26 U.S.C. §988 — govinfo.gov (U.S. Government Publishing Office) verified; IRS Publication 525 — irs.gov verified; Cornell LII (U.S. Code mirror) — law.cornell.edu verified
